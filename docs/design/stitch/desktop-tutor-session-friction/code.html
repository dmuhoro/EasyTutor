Why doesn't the apparent contact area affect the limiting frictional force on dry brake pads? Intuition says larger shoes should generate far higher stopping resistance.

Your intuition is extremely common among apprentice engineers, but classical dry friction behaves counter-intuitively because of the sharp distinction between apparent macroscopic area and true microscopic contact area.

Governing Formulation • Coulomb-Amontons Law Eq. 15.3
F_max = μ_s × R_n Independent of Area (A)

Notice that the nominal contact area (A) does not appear in the equation. The maximum limiting frictional force (F_max) depends purely on the static friction coefficient (μ_s) and the normal reaction force (R_n).

The Microscopic Asperity Mechanism

Machined brake rotor surfaces and lining materials are not atomically smooth; they consist of microscopic peaks and valleys called asperities. Solid-to-solid contact occurs only where opposing asperities touch and cold-weld under extreme local stress.

balance
The self-compensating trade-off: If you double the apparent pad area under the same clamping clamp load, the pressure per unit area is cut exactly in half. Hence, half the microscopic junctions form per mm², leaving the net sum of true microscopic contact area precisely unaltered .

We use wider pads in automotive discs not to increase initial friction, but to manage thermal dissipation and reduce wear rates over repeated brake applications.
